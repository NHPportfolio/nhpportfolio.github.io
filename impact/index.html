Inside the Source Governance Engine · From My Capstone Presentation

How It Works

  1. 1HighlightThe journalist selects a passage in their own byline. The unit of capture is what they point at, not every named entity.
  2. 2Match and inferAI matches the source against the vetted repository and infers metadata from the passage: source type, power position, on or off the record.
  3. 3Save snapshotTagged sources flow into a dashboard, where patterns surface across stories: institutional or community, recurring sources, gaps.

What I Learned

The form factor was the problem, not the features.

v0 · Standalone Web App

26

named entities extracted from one article, every name treated as a source. Even with a polished form, going through 26 cards was tedious.

v1 · Browser Extension

4–6

sources the journalist actually used, captured by highlighting passages. The AI suggests; the journalist confirms.

Tools journalists actually use are infrastructure, not destinations. Standalone apps require commitment; ambient tools live alongside existing work.

Ethical Questions I Worked Through

  1. Source consent doesn't transfer. A source agreed to one reporter and one story, not to a searchable database. A required consent field means the reader-facing tool cannot surface anyone who wasn't on the record.
  2. A transparency dashboard can be weaponized. "78% institutional sources" in bad-faith hands becomes "this newsroom is biased." Dashboards stay per newsroom, with reporters compared only to themselves.
  3. AI inference of source type is a power claim. The model decides whether someone is "institutional" or "an advocate," so the AI only suggests. Every suggestion is visible and editable, and the journalist makes the call.
  4. Demographic auto-tagging is possible. I chose not to ship it. The field is reserved but stays off until it has had its own design conversation.
  5. A reader-facing tool could repeat the narrowness it reveals. The next stage must show readers whose voices ground each answer, not just the answer.
